# Chapter 9

Joseph's farm and carpenter's workshop were located a little outside Nazareth in a valley divided in the middle by a small stream. On the way there, one passed an abandoned farm with dilapidated stables, an overgrown meadow and a withered rose garden. Joseph's farm appeared to be the exact opposite in comparison. Well-tended trees, a large, mown meadow and the farm seemed to form a harmonious whole. In a curved floor, the path led up to the courtyard. Roses of various colours grew to the left and right of the path. The first building on the left was a stable where an ox and a donkey were contentedly eating hay from a manger side by side. Next to it was the carpenter's workshop, built in wood, with two floors, and next to it some orderly piles of wooden boards and beams, piles of nails, roots and anything else that could possibly be used for anything. The actual dwelling house was on the right and behind it one could see a taller, slender wooden house with a tower, the temple cottage as Joseph called it. A curved stone path led to it.


In front of the carpenter's workshop was a workbench on which Klophas, a boy of about 15, had clamped a rocking horse that he was working on with a file. Every few moments he stepped back and looked at a part of the horse from different angles, got down on his knees or bent over, felt it and then made a few strokes with the file again. The door behind him opened and Josef came out. He stood behind Klophas and watched what Klophas' hands were doing until he noticed. He turned around abruptly, stood up straight and looked at Josef. He smiled at me, clasped the horse with his hands and closed his eyes. Then he stroked his hands all around the horse, turned to Klophas and nodded at him. Klophas's heart began to pound. "Yes?" he asked excitedly. "Yes.", Josef answered. Klophas: "But some parts are not quite in the flow yet." "Yes. But I think you know what's missing in each one." Klophas laughed: "Then after that I can oil it?" "When you're done with the missing parts, you can oil it." "Yes! And then I'll paint ..." Joseph laughed and put a hand on his shoulder, "Come! It's time to pray."


Klophas carefully put the file down on the workbench and followed Joseph along the stone path behind the dwelling house. He entered the temple cottage after him. Inside was a large room that reached to the roof. On the highest side was an altar wall with many different pictures, glowing stones, painted wooden figures and small torches everywhere in between. With a flint, Joseph lit a dry brushwood and with it the various torches until the whole wall was brightly lit.


Joseph knelt down, Klophas beside him. Joseph prayed:
"My Lord and God!
You were with the God in the beginnings,
Thou didst order time and eternity,
Thou hast made all things that were made, and nothing was made but by Thee.
In you is life, and life is the light of men.
And your light shines in the darkness, but the darkness has not taken hold of it.
Send us the help we need,
To find us in the darkness, To see and meet the other.
Give us the hand with which we can find the way to the light and to the power,
to the word, out of our own will.
Amen."
"Amen," repeated Clophas.
Then both placed their foreheads on the floor for a few minutes and paused in silence.

Joseph rose, Klophas following him. They bowed against the altar wall and each other and Klophas turned towards the door. Almost at the exit, he glanced once more at the wall. His eye caught on a small wooden figure lying in a compartment in the lower right-hand corner of the wall, with a beautifully shining blue stone beside it.
He paused, "Why is this figure lying down? All the others are standing."
Joseph calmly replied, "Because it never learned to stand."
Klophas: "This is a child that died young?"
Joseph was struck by pain and closed his eyes.
After a while he opened them again and said to Klophas: "Yes, exactly.
He never learned to stand.
He was never born."


Klophas turned to the door and walked out. Joseph slid onto his knees and bent forward. A deep pain ran through his stomach. He breathed in and out and suddenly he found himself on a high rock with a wide view. He looked down at himself and saw the body of a boy, perhaps two years older than Klophas. He spread his arms, raised his head to the sky and started to send a scream with everything he had to the horizon. Then he heard a girl's voice behind him: "King Joseph!" He turned around. A girl's voice here in solitude? How was that possible? But sure enough, there she stood, Talia, or so he thought, the daughter of a family passing through. He had seen the family a few days before at their tent, by the stream, just before the lake. And there he had seen her too, as he would never forget her again in his life. She was bathing with her sister and when they noticed him, she smiled at him and he froze. She smiled more clearly, her mouth opened slightly and just as he was about to run away, she rose from the water and stood in front of him, a stone's throw away, completely naked. He looked and looked, he couldn't look away. She stopped and looked him in the eye. He couldn't move and it was only when their mother called her and she turned away that he was able to break free of the spell and ran away. That was Talia, or something. And now here she was.
"King Joseph," she said again, coming further up the rock.
"I'm not a king," he called to her.
"But you will be one one day!"
"There have been no kings in Israel since the Romans came."
"My father means that there are still kings, and that the Romans cannot forbid that there should be kings. And that you are one ..."
She was now standing two metres in front of him and went down on her knees with a grin.
"Don't give me that nonsense! I'm not a king and I'll never be one!"
"If you say so, then it's true. You are a king, after all." She laughed, pulled a serious face and said in a dark voice, "And I am a nobleman and I want to get a beautiful land from you to rule over. That is why I kneel here before you."
He took her hand and pulled her up, "Stand up. It is not proper for a nobleman to mock the king!" He smiled and she stood before him. A warm current ran through him, she stood so close to him and even came a little closer. His his breath caught and heart pounded as it had by the stream a few days ago.
"Well, king, are you afraid of me? I see your heart beating."
"Don't you see. He clasped his chest with both hands and stood erect."
"Your hands are trembling."
He looked at them, they were trembling slightly, "Not at all. Maybe a tiny bit ..."
She pulled one hand towards her and looked at it, "Yes they are, I can see it quite clearly as they are trembling." Then she suddenly gripped the hand harder and pressed it against her chest, "And now? Are they trembling even more now?"
He pulled it away with force and looked at her in amazement.
"Wasn't that nice?"
He tried to shake his head but couldn't manage it. He looked spellbound at her chest.
"Wasn't that beautiful?" she asked again, pulling her shirt aside with one hand so that he could see a bosom.
Heart beating to his throat, he turned to leave when she called out, "Aren't you even going to touch them?"
He closed his eyes and a few moments later he turned and came to her. He stroked his hand under her shirt and looked her in the eyes. She beamed at him, closed her eyes and he felt a force run through his whole body. He pulled her to him with strength and kissed her. They sank down together.


Six weeks later, Joseph stood in front of his father, sitting on the family chair, a kind of throne, only it wasn't allowed to be called that. Next to him on the left and right sat his mother and uncle. Next to the uncle was his cousin, two years older. The father asked in a strong voice, "Joseph, my eldest, what happened?"
Josef looked him straight in the eye but did not answer.
The father repeated his question, "What happened? Could it be that she has the child from you?"
Josef remained silent, thinking of Talia, how they had watched the sunset together, arm in arm, and then he had taken her to the stream where her parents had their tent. She had looked at him so urgently when he said goodbye, so freely, and then she had smiled, in her way, as only she could smile. She was such a strong woman, maybe two years older than him. No, that was sacred, that was hers. He could not betray that.
Father: "I won't ask a third time!"
Mother walked up to him and held his cheeks, "Joseph, if she has a son, he could be your heir to the throne. He'll be the eldest. And you can't do that."
Joseph looked at her.
She continued, "It won't work. She is not a Jew. That would break the royal line. We are descendants of David. David. Joseph, do you hear me?"
Joseph nodded slightly, he understood well what his mother was saying, but as king he also had to always follow his heart, he could never lose trust in his heart, otherwise he would become a dark king. And he was never allowed to lie. His father had told him that. He was not allowed to talk about it either, because this was a secret among kings. He looked at his father. He did not seem to press him like his mother or his uncle.
Joseph's uncle stood up and said to his mother, "He is not yet born, so he cannot yet be heir to the throne." He looked at her urgently.
She shook her head. "No, not in this generation. If we can avoid that ..."
The uncle, "If he is Joseph's son, then we cannot avoid it."
Joseph looked up in alarm, "What can't be avoided?"
Everyone fell silent. Josef's cousin interrupted the silence, "Cousin, there are ways to prevent the child from being born, or born alive."
Joseph shouted loudly, "No! That would be interfering with destiny. If he is the heir to the throne, what would it be if we killed him? It would already be treason if we do not do everything possible to protect his life. Father?"
Father looked at him and remained silent. There was something dark in his eyes.
Joseph: "No, no, no! We won't do that."
"That is not your decision. I am the king," his father said calmly.
Uncle: "In his mother's womb he is not yet heir to the throne, the protection doesn't apply there. That was established once five or six generations ago in our family. Rabbi ... I have forgotten his name."
Mother: "Gonispur. That led to deep upheavals at that time. I don't want that in our generation."
Father: "My son, if you don't say anything about it, we will assume that you were with her."
Joseph: "But --- but you cannot pass judgement without knowing what was. Father!"
Father: "No, I can't. But I can accept it, for all of us."
Joseph: "And then what happens?"
The father was silent.
There was a loud knock, three times on the door at the other end of the room. Everyone looked there. A servant opened and said loudly:
"The family transiting river dwellers."
Joseph: "They have a name too, don't they? What is their name?"
At that moment Talia stepped out of the door and walked with sure steps towards Joseph. Her parents, visibly shy, followed slowly behind her.
She stopped about five metres from Josef and waved a hand, "Hello!"
Josef froze. He had expected the parents, not her. But that was how she was, she did what she wanted and everyone let her do it.
He cautiously raised his hand and groaned out an almost inaudible "Hello!". Then he turned to his father, who looked at him in amazement. He turned to her again. She came closer, he wanted to shout at her to stop, but it was too late. She held her hands in front of him and went down on her knees. He took them, trembling, and said to her softly, "Flee! I'll find you." She looked at him in amazement and confusion, but continued to hold his hand.
The father had got up. Uncle and cousin both had their hand on the sword and were looking at him.
Joseph turned around: "I revoke my succession to the throne! There is no need to harm her!"
The father shouted to the guards to take everyone out of the room and two guards each took Talia, her mother and her father. Talia tried to hold on for a moment, but was yanked free by one of the guards. The doors slammed shut. There was silence in the room.
"I have revoked my succession! You must let her go," Joseph shouted once more.
The father sat down on the family chair and signalled to Joseph to come to him. He followed and knelt before the father.
"You can revoke your succession to the throne. That is your right. I will give you one week to think it over before your decision becomes valid. You will spend that time in your room."
"But you cannot harm the family during that time. Promise me!"
The father was silent.
From the side, his uncle said, "You can revoke the succession for yourself. But not for your son."
With his finger, the father instructed two servants to escort the son to his room.
"NO!" cried Joseph as a servant grabbed him by the shoulder, "NO, you will not hurt them!" He broke away and looked to his father. He repeated his sign and the servants took Joseph out of his room.
He awoke in the temple cottage to a picture of the bloody campsite by the stream. His face was drenched in sweat. He was breathing heavily. A quiet voice said from the side, "Clophas is your new apprentice." He turned to the voice and saw an angel with legs comfortably crossed in a funny and very beautiful robe of green, red and blue tones looking at him with warm eyes. "Only angels can look like that," he thought, and he felt his tension fall from him and his breathing become calm. He smiled back and nodded. The angel disappeared, Joseph dried his sweat with a cloth and stepped out of the temple cottage. He went to Klophas, who was oiling the rocking horse with a cloth, and stood in front of him. He put away the cloth, straightened up and Joseph said:
"I take you into my service as my apprentice. Is that what you want?"
Klopha's heart began to pound, he wanted to say something but couldn't right away. After a few moments he produced a half "Yes." "So soon? I've only been there six weeks ..." "Enough," Josef replied and smiled, then he went into the carpenter's workshop.
No sooner had the door slammed shut than Klophas jumped up, ran out into the meadow, jumped onto a large stone from which one could see the valley and the plain in front of it, and spread his arms and shouted as loud as he could out of joy.
